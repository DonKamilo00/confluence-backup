Toward Neuro-Adaptive Productivity: Advanced AI Architectures for ADHD Management

Executive Summary

The digital productivity landscape is currently undergoing a paradigm shift, moving from static, user-initiated task management tools to dynamic, neuro-adaptive systems that actively regulate the user's environment and cognitive state. This transition is particularly critical for the domain of Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) assistive technology. Current market solutions typically offer "simple goal decomposition" or gamified lists, yet these tools often fail to address the core neurocognitive deficits of ADHD: temporal blindness, executive dysfunction regarding task initiation, and emotional dysregulation triggered by environmental overwhelm.

This report, commissioned for the startup "myndmap," provides an exhaustive analysis of novel AI development directions. It synthesizes findings from over 370 research snippets spanning Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), computer vision, digital phenotyping, and reinforcement learning. The central thesis is that the next generation of ADHD tools must function as a "digital prosthetic" for the prefrontal cortex—a system that does not merely record tasks but actively perceives the physical and digital obstacles preventing their completion.

The analysis is structured around three primary pillars of innovation. First, we explore Computer Vision for Environmental Regulation, detailing how mobile-optimized algorithms can quantify visual clutter ("doom piles") and track gaze patterns to detect attention lapses in real-time. Second, we investigate Passive Sensing and Digital Phenotyping, demonstrating how smartphone accelerometers and keystroke dynamics can objectify hyperactivity and monitor medication efficacy without active user input. Third, we propose a framework for Context-Aware Interruption Management, utilizing Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDP) to optimize the timing of notifications, thereby reducing the "switching cost" that paralyzes neurodivergent users. By integrating these technologies into a unified mobile architecture, "myndmap" can transition from a passive tool to an active, empathetic partner in the user's daily life.

1. The Theoretical Framework of AI-Mediated Executive Function

1.1 The Limitations of Static Productivity Models and the "Wall of Awful"

Contemporary productivity applications largely operate on a "user-pull" model, assuming the user possesses the executive function necessary to initiate, prioritize, and sustain attention on tasks once they are digitally cataloged. For the neurodivergent brain, specifically in ADHD, the deficit is not in knowing what to do, but in the execution—a gap bridged by executive functions such as inhibition, working memory, and set-shifting.1 The reliance on static lists often exacerbates what clinical psychologists term the "Wall of Awful"—a psychological barrier where the visual accumulation of uncompleted tasks creates emotional dysregulation, shame, and subsequent task paralysis.1 While the "simple goal decomposition" currently implemented in "myndmap" addresses the planning deficit by breaking large projects into steps, it fails to address the initiation and monitoring deficits that prevent users from taking the first step.

Recent surveys in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) highlight that digital work environments often amplify challenges such as time blindness and emotional reactivity for adults with ADHD.2 Conventional tools fail to support the cognitive variability and overload experienced by neurodivergent professionals, who often require low-touch, adaptive interventions rather than high-maintenance data entry systems.2 The novel direction for "myndmap" must therefore be the transition to a Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) Cyber-Physical System.2 In this framework, the mobile device acts as an external cortex, sensing the user's state (e.g., "Is the user hyperactive?", "Is the environment cluttered?") and intervening dynamically to regulate the feedback loops that shape attention.

1.2 Neuro-Adaptive Architecture: A Systems Thinking Approach

A neuro-adaptive system is defined by its ability to modify its interaction model based on the user's real-time cognitive load and emotional state. This requires a shift from explicit input (user typing a task) to implicit input (system sensing user behavior). The proposed architecture relies on a "privacy-first adaptive agent" model.2 This agent senses cues such as tab usage, application focus, and physical inactivity using on-device Machine Learning (ML). These cues are then utilized to infer attention states and deliver nudges, reflective prompts, or accountability-based presence (body doubling) that aid regulation without causing disruption.2

This systems thinking approach views attention not as a static resource but as a dynamic variable shaped by feedback loops.2 For example, if a user is stuck in a "doom scroll" loop on social media, a neuro-adaptive system would not just block the app (a rigid intervention) but might first check the user's physiological state via keystroke dynamics or accelerometer data. If the data suggests high stress, the intervention might be a "breathing exercise" prompt rather than a "get back to work" notification. This nuance is critical; interventions must be context-aware to avoid triggering defiance or anxiety, common traits in ADHD known as Pathological Demand Avoidance (PDA).3

Furthermore, the architecture must support "low-resource settings" to ensure accessibility.5 While advanced AI models are computationally intensive, the goal is to develop state-of-the-art models that address specific challenges of diverse healthcare contexts, fostering solutions that address needs in both high- and low-resource regions.5 This implies a reliance on "Edge AI"—processing data locally on the smartphone to preserve privacy and reduce latency, a crucial consideration given the sensitivity of mental health data.2

The integration of these systems requires a rigorous evaluation of "Data-Centric vs. Algorithm-Centric Approaches".5 While better algorithms (like Transformers) are valuable, for ADHD digital phenotyping, the quality and context of the data (e.g., separating "fidgeting" from "walking") are often more determinative of success. Thus, the architecture proposed in this report emphasizes robust data ingestion pipelines that fuse multimodal signals—vision, motion, and interaction logs—to create a holistic view of the user's executive function.

2. Computer Vision for Environmental and Attentional Regulation

Computer vision (CV) offers the most significant untapped potential for ADHD productivity applications. By utilizing the smartphone camera—both the rear-facing world camera and the front-facing user camera—the application can extend its utility beyond the screen to manage the user's physical environment and physiological attention state. This section details how CV can be deployed to objectively quantify environmental clutter (a major barrier to task initiation) and monitor gaze patterns to detect distraction in real-time.

2.1 Quantifying "Doom Piles": Algorithms for Visual Clutter Detection

One of the most debilitating aspects of ADHD is the inability to organize physical space, leading to visual clutter that further degrades executive function. This phenomenon, often colloquially referred to as "doom piles," creates a feedback loop where the mess itself becomes a visual distractor, consuming attentional resources and causing "clutter blindness".1 The brain is overwhelmed by visual stimuli, preventing the executive function required to initiate cleaning tasks. To build a novel "De-cluttering Assistant," the system must objectively measure "messiness" or visual clutter.

2.1.1 Feature Congestion and Subband Entropy: The Metrics of Mess

Research in visual perception offers two robust algorithms suitable for quantifying clutter: Feature Congestion and Subband Entropy.7 The Feature Congestion measure posits that clutter is a function of the difficulty of adding a new item to a scene that would be reliably noticed. It involves calculating the local covariance of features—such as color, luminance, and orientation—at multiple scales.7 High covariance implies high clutter, as the visual field is saturated with competing features. Conversely, Subband Entropy relates clutter to the density of visual information. It is calculated by decomposing the image into different frequency bands (typically using wavelets or Gaussian pyramids) and calculating the Shannon entropy of the coefficients.7

A mobile implementation would utilize a lightweight CV pipeline to process video frames from the rear camera. The process begins by converting the input image into the perceptually based CIELab color space, which aligns more closely with human vision than RGB.7 The system then generates a Gaussian pyramid, alternately smoothing and subsampling the image to analyze it at multiple scales (e.g., fine details vs. broad shapes).7 For each scale, features such as luminance contrast and orientation are extracted. The "clutter map" is then derived by computing the local variance or entropy of these features.7

2.1.2 Semantic Scene Understanding and Micro-Task Generation

While measuring clutter density is useful, the system must also understand what is cluttering the space to provide actionable advice. This requires Semantic Scene Understanding using object detection models. YOLO (You Only Look Once) architectures, specifically YOLO-Nano or MobileNet-YOLO variants, are optimized for mobile devices and can perform real-time object detection with minimal latency.9 These models are trained end-to-end to predict bounding boxes and class probabilities in a single pass, making them efficient enough for real-time video analysis on smartphones.11

In cluttered indoor environments, standard object detection often fails due to occlusion—objects hiding behind one another. Research into multi-view 3D object recognition suggests that integrating information from multiple viewpoints can robustly recognize objects even in clutter.13 By having the user pan their camera across the room, the app can build a probabilistic map of objects, utilizing Structure from Motion (SfM) to understand the 3D spatial layout.14 This allows the system to distinguish between a "pile of laundry" and a "sleeping pet," or to identify "dishes" amidst "papers."

This semantic understanding enables Micro-Task Decomposition. Instead of the overwhelming instruction "Clean the room," the CV system can generate a sequence of specific, manageable tasks:

  1. Detection: System identifies 3 mugs, a pile of laundry, and scattered papers.

  2. Prioritization: Using a logic of "hygiene first," it prioritizes the mugs.

  3. Instruction: "Let's start small. I see 3 mugs. Grab them and put them in the sink."
    This approach directly targets the executive dysfunction of "initiation" by removing the cognitive load of planning and prioritization.1 The system acts as an external prefrontal cortex, parsing the chaotic visual field into a linear sequence of actions.

2.1.3 The "Heatmap of Chaos": Gamification via Augmented Reality

To further reduce the psychological barrier to cleaning, the application can leverage Augmented Reality (AR). By overlaying the computed clutter map onto the live camera feed, the app can create a "Heatmap of Chaos." High-entropy areas (e.g., the messy desk) can be highlighted in red, while organized areas appear green.16 This gamifies the cleaning process: as the user tidies a specific zone, they can watch the "heat" dissipate in real-time, providing the immediate dopamine reward that the ADHD brain craves but often fails to generate endogenously.16

Furthermore, clustering algorithms like K-Means can be applied to color-code detected objects, suggesting grouping strategies (e.g., "Gather all the blue items first").16 This technique, known as "clustering," is a valid cognitive strategy for organizing, and the AR overlay can visualize it, effectively "highlighting" the path to a cleaner room. This integration of CV, AR, and gamification represents a novel, high-impact feature for "myndmap" that extends far beyond simple list-making.

2.2 Front-Facing Camera: Attention Monitoring and Gaze Estimation

ADHD is characterized by attention lapses, high distractibility, and difficulty sustaining focus on non-stimulating tasks. Mobile Appearance-Based Gaze Estimation allows the front-facing camera to track where the user is looking without the need for specialized infrared hardware, enabling the system to monitor attention in real-time.18

2.2.1 Gaze Fixation, Saccades, and Distraction Detection

Research indicates that individuals with ADHD exhibit distinct eye movement patterns compared to neurotypical controls. Specifically, they show significantly poorer performance in prosaccade and antisaccade tasks, with increased saccadic frequency and reduced fixation stability.20 They also tend to have shorter fixation durations on target stimuli and are more easily drawn to visual distractors.21

By implementing a lightweight gaze estimation model like I2DNet or MPIIFaceGaze 18, the app can track the user's gaze vector relative to the screen. The I2DNet architecture, for instance, is designed to improve subject-independent gaze estimation accuracy, achieving state-of-the-art performance on mobile datasets.18

  • Metric: The system calculates a Fixation Duration Threshold. If the user's gaze deviates from the screen (or a designated work area) for a duration exceeding a set threshold (e.g., >15 seconds), the system infers a "Distraction Event".23

  • Analysis: The system can differentiate between "micro-distractions" (quick glances away) and "sustained disengagement" (looking at a TV or another person). Frequent saccades off-screen might indicate restlessness or boredom, while prolonged fixation elsewhere suggests a loss of focus.22

This data can be presented to the user as a "Focus Quality" score, helping them understand their own attentional rhythms. For example, the app might reveal that the user's gaze stability degrades significantly after 20 minutes of work, suggesting that they should switch to a Pomodoro cycle with 20-minute intervals rather than 25 or 30.20

2.2.2 Multimodal Emotion and Fatigue Detection

Appearance-based methods can also perform multi-task learning to detect emotional states and fatigue from the video feed. ADHD is often accompanied by emotional dysregulation, and frustration can quickly lead to task abandonment. Facial expression analysis can detect signs of frustration (e.g., brow furrowing, lip compression) or fatigue (e.g., increased blink rate, slow eyelid closure).24

Integrating these signals allows for a nuanced understanding of the user's state. For instance, multimodal fusion of gaze and facial expression can distinguish between different types of non-work behavior:

  • Boredom: Characterized by looking away from the screen combined with a neutral or slack facial expression.

  • Overwhelm/Frustration: Characterized by looking away combined with signs of distress or agitation.

  • Fatigue: Characterized by looking at the screen but with a high blink rate or "droopy" eyelids.

This distinction is critical for the AI agent to provide the correct intervention.18 If the user is bored, the agent might suggest a "gamified" challenge to increase stimulation. If they are overwhelmed, the agent might suggest a breathing exercise or breaking the task down further. If they are fatigued, the agent might suggest a restorative break.

3. Digital Phenotyping and Passive Sensing

Digital Phenotyping refers to the "moment-by-moment quantification of the individual-level human phenotype in situ using data from personal digital devices".28 This field offers a revolutionary capability for ADHD management: the objective measurement of symptom severity and medication efficacy without requiring active user input (which users with ADHD often struggle to provide consistently). By leveraging the smartphone's embedded sensors—accelerometers, gyroscopes, and touchscreens—"myndmap" can build a continuous, objective profile of the user's neurocognitive state.

3.1 Accelerometry and Hyperactivity Quantification

Hyperactivity is a core symptom of ADHD, yet it is traditionally assessed through subjective self-reports or observation, which are prone to bias and recall error. Smartphone accelerometers and gyroscopes allow for the continuous, high-frequency collection of motor activity data, providing an objective measure of "restlessness".30

3.1.1 Variance and Zero-Crossing Rate (ZCR): The Mathematics of Fidgeting

Research confirms that specific features of accelerometer data can robustly distinguish individuals with ADHD from neurotypical controls. Specifically, the variance (magnitude of movement) and Zero-Crossing Rate (ZCR) (frequency of movement) are significantly higher in individuals with ADHD, particularly during tasks requiring sedentary focus.32

To quantify this, the system should calculate the Vector Magnitude (VM) of the accelerometer data, combining the x, y, and z axes:

$$VM = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$$

34

High variance in VM while the user is supposedly "stationary" (e.g., location services place them at their work desk, or the calendar indicates a work block) indicates restlessness, fidgeting, or squirming—classic markers of hyperactive-impulsive symptoms.33 Furthermore, graph-theoretical methods applied to actigraph data have shown that measures like "bridges" in the time series graph can distinguish between morning and evening activity patterns in ADHD patients, offering a nuanced view of diurnal variation.35

3.1.2 The "Medication Efficacy" Monitor: Digital Pharmacodynamics

A major challenge for adults with ADHD is medication management—specifically, knowing if a dose is effective, when it peaks, and when it wears off ("the crash"). Subjective perception of these states is often inaccurate due to poor interoception.

  • Novel Application: The app can track motor variance relative to the time of medication intake (logged by the user). Research indicates that a statistically significant decrease in accelerometer variance and ZCR correlates with the onset of stimulant action (e.g., methylphenidate).30 Conversely, a spike in variance in the late afternoon can objectively signal the medication wearing off.

  • Visualization: "myndmap" can present the user with a "Focus & Calm" graph, overlaying their "Motor Restlessness" curve with their medication window. If the data shows restlessness spiking at 4:00 PM, the user has objective, longitudinal data to discuss dosage or timing adjustments with their psychiatrist.38 This feature elevates the app from a simple productivity tool to a clinical companion that supports evidence-based treatment optimization.

3.2 Keystroke Dynamics for Stress and Cognitive Load

How a user types is as revealing as what they type. Keystroke dynamics—the timing and pressure patterns of typing—serve as potent biomarkers for cognitive load, stress, and emotional state.40

  • Stress and Fatigue Detection: Decreased processing speed and irregular typing rhythms (e.g., variable flight times between keys) correlate with cognitive fatigue and high stress.41 Users with ADHD often demonstrate higher variability in typing latency, particularly when their attention begins to wane.42 Detecting this variability allows the app to suggest a break before the user reaches burnout.

  • Frustration Modeling: Touchscreen sensors can also measure "thump" pressure (inferred via the surface area of the touch) and the velocity of backspace usage. Rapid, forceful typing combined with frequent backspacing is a strong indicator of frustration.25

  • Privacy-First Implementation: Crucially, this analysis can be performed using only metadata (timestamps of key-down and key-up events, touch coordinates) without ever recording the linguistic content of what is typed. This "metadata-only" approach is essential for maintaining user privacy while still extracting rich behavioral insights.40

3.3 Multimodal Fusion for "State Estimation"

The true power of digital phenotyping lies in Multimodal Fusion. No single sensor tells the whole story, but combining them allows for robust "State Estimation." A "State Estimation Engine" would fuse disparate data streams to construct a real-time model of the user's condition:

  1. Accelerometer Data: Is the user physically restless or calm? 32

  2. Keystroke Data: Is the user typing fluently or erratically? 42

  3. Screen Usage: Is the user rapid-switching between apps (a marker of digital distractibility) or engaging in long sessions in a single app (hyperfocus)? 42

  4. Ambient Light & Context: Is the user in a dark room late at night (risk of revenge bedtime procrastination)? 42

Research Opportunity: Developing a "Digital ADHD Biomarker Index" that correlates these fused sensor inputs with standardized rating scales like the SNAP-IV (for symptoms) or the ASRS (Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale).30 A study demonstrating that "myndmap's" sensor fusion algorithm correlates with clinical symptom severity would be a landmark publication, validating the tool as a digital therapeutic rather than just a lifestyle app.45

4. Reinforcement Learning for Context-Aware Interruption Management

Users with ADHD are uniquely vulnerable to interruptions. A notification that might be helpful to a neurotypical user can derail an ADHD user for hours due to the high "switching cost" associated with the disorder. The interruption breaks the fragile state of focus, and re-engaging with the task can be monumentally difficult. Therefore, "myndmap" must use Reinforcement Learning (RL) to move beyond static reminders (e.g., "Remind me at 9:00 AM") to Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAI) that learn when the user is receptive to engagement.48

4.1 The Problem: Static vs. Adaptive Notifications

Static notification systems operate on fixed time or location triggers. However, 9:00 AM might be the only time the user has achieved a state of "hyperfocus." Interrupting this state with a "Drink Water" reminder is counterproductive and creates annoyance. Conversely, a user might be stuck in a "doom scrolling" loop at 11:00 PM, a prime moment for an intervention to suggest sleep. The goal of an RL system is to predict the "interruptibility" of the user and deliver the right message at the right time.50

4.2 Designing the RL Agent: A POMDP Formulation

The problem of notification scheduling can be mathematically modeled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP).52 The system cannot perfectly know the user's mental state (it is "partially observable"), but it can infer a belief state from the available sensor data.

4.2.1 State Space ($S$)

The state vector ($S$) represents the environment and the user's context. For a mobile implementation, this must be minimal enough for efficient processing but rich enough to capture nuance.54 Based on the research, the recommended feature set includes:

  • Time Context: Hour of day, day of week.

  • Device State: Is the screen on? What is the battery level? Which app category is foregrounded (e.g., Productivity vs. Social Media)?.56

  • Physical Context: Location (GPS/WiFi semantic tag like "Home" or "Office"), Activity state (Still, Walking, Driving inferred from accelerometer).57

  • Inferred Cognitive State: Recent stress level (from keystroke dynamics), "Restlessness" score (from accelerometer variance).57

4.2.2 Action Space ($A$)

The agent must choose an action ($A$) based on the current state. The action space includes:

  • Send Notification Immediately: Deliver the prompt now.

  • Snooze/Queue: Withhold the notification and queue it for a later "batch" delivery or a more opportune moment.

  • Silent Update: Update the app's UI or widget without triggering a vibration or sound.49

  • Modulate Intensity: Choose the type of notification (e.g., a gentle nudge vs. a loud alarm).

4.2.3 Reward Function ($R$)

The reward function ($R$) is the critical component that guides the agent's learning. It quantifies the success of an action.59 For an ADHD app, the reward function must penalize interruptions that cause stress or distraction.

A proposed reward function structure is:

$$ R_t = \alpha \cdot \mathbb{I}(\text{ActionTaken}) + \beta \cdot \mathbb{I}(\text{TaskComplete}) - \gamma \cdot \mathbb{I}(\text{Dismissal}) - \delta \cdot \text{StressLevel} $$

Where:

  • $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is an indicator function (1 if true, 0 if false).

  • Positive Rewards (+): The user taps the notification ($\text{ActionTaken}$) or completes the associated task within a set window ($\text{TaskComplete}$). Explicit feedback (e.g., user rating "This was helpful") also yields a high reward.

  • Negative Rewards (-): The user swipes away the notification immediately ($\text{Dismissal}$) or ignores it.

  • Contextual Penalties: Crucially, if the user's StressLevel (measured via keystrokes) spikes immediately after the notification, or if they unlock the phone but open a distraction app (induced distraction), the agent receives a heavy penalty ($\delta$). This teaches the agent not to interrupt when the user is stressed or prone to distraction.48

4.3 Deep Q-Networks (DQN) for Personalized Policy Learning

To solve this POMDP, a Deep Q-Network (DQN) can be employed. The DQN approximates the optimal action-value function ($Q$-function), mapping states to the best possible actions.62

  • Personalization: The model starts with a baseline policy (e.g., "don't interrupt during meetings") but progressively learns the individual user's preferences. For example, it might learn that User A responds well to reminders during their morning commute (detected via GPS and motion), while User B ignores everything until they are at their desk.

  • Offline Learning: To preserve battery and privacy, the model can be trained using "offline reinforcement learning" on logged interaction data, or utilizing Federated Learning to share generalized policy improvements across the user base without sharing raw personal data.61

  • Implementation: The agent can be deployed using mobile-optimized ML libraries (e.g., TensorFlow Lite). The "State-Marginalized Importance Sampling" approach can be used to evaluate the policy's effectiveness offline before deploying it live, ensuring that the AI doesn't annoy the user while it is learning.61

By implementing this RL framework, "myndmap" transforms from a "nagging" app into a considerate digital assistant that respects the user's cognitive resources.

5. Generative AI and the "Virtual Body Double"

"Body Doubling"—the practice of working alongside another person to facilitate productivity—is a well-established and highly effective coping strategy for ADHD.2 The presence of another person provides a "social anchor," increasing accountability and reducing the anxiety of task initiation. However, human body doubles are not always available or affordable. Generative AI, specifically Large Language Models (LLMs), allows for the creation of Always-On Virtual Companions that simulate this supportive presence.

5.1 Beyond Text: Presence, Agency, and Social Mirroring

Current chatbots are primarily reactive and text-based. A true "Virtual Body Double" must be proactive and simulate presence and agency. Research suggests that an AI agent that exhibits autonomous behavior—such as "talking to itself" or commenting on the shared environment—creates a stronger sense of social presence than a simple command-response bot.65

Novel Feature: The "Co-Working Avatar"

"myndmap" should implement a visual avatar (or a minimalist, abstract visual representation) that "works" alongside the user.

  • Mechanism: When the user initiates a "Focus Session" (e.g., for 25 minutes), the Avatar also "starts working."

  • Generative Self-Talk: Using an LLM, the avatar generates "self-talk" captions that reflect its own "effort." For example, if the user is writing, the avatar might display captions like: "Okay, sorting this data is tough, but I'm making progress," or "Phew, almost got distracted there. Back to it.".65

  • Psychological Hook: This leverages Social Mirroring. Seeing the agent "struggle" and "focus" validates the user's own effort and normalizes the difficulty of the task, reducing the isolation of executive dysfunction.65 The agent becomes a peer rather than a supervisor.

5.2 Persona Design: The CBT-Informed Coach

The conversational style of the AI is paramount. Standard AI assistants are designed to be servile and efficient. An ADHD coach, however, needs to be "empathetic but firm," employing techniques from Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT).67

System Prompt Engineering:

The LLM's system prompt must be explicitly grounded in CBT principles to avoid enabling avoidance behaviors or increasing overwhelm.

  • Instruction: "You are an expert ADHD coach. Do not simply solve the task for the user. Instead, use Socratic questioning to help them identify blocks. If they express overwhelm, use validation techniques before suggesting a micro-step. Focus on 'initiation' over 'completion'."

  • Example Interaction:

  • User: "I can't start this report. It's too much."

  • Bad AI (Standard): "Here is an outline for the report." (This solves the problem but doesn't build the skill).

  • Good AI (CBT): "It sounds like that report feels really heavy right now. That makes sense. What is the very first, tiniest step you can do? Is it just opening the document? Let's just do that.".69

This persona design ensures the AI acts as a therapeutic tool, helping the user build "mental muscle" for tackling future tasks.

5.3 Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) for Contextual Coaching

To advance the "simple goal decomposition" currently in the app, the system should integrate the Vision and Sensing layers into the LLM's context window via Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG). The AI should not just know what the task is, but the context in which the user is trying to do it.

  • Contextual Prompting: The prompt sent to the LLM would include sensor data: "The user wants to 'clean the kitchen.' Vision data detects high clutter (Entropy 8.5) with 'dishes' and 'trash' identified. Accelerometer shows the user has been sedentary for 3 hours. Generate a decomposition that starts with the easiest physical action to break inertia.".71

  • Output: Instead of a generic list, the AI might say: "The kitchen looks like a big job right now. Since you've been sitting for a while, let's just stand up. Find one piece of trash to throw away. Just one. Can you do that?"

This integration of sensing and generative text creates a deeply personalized coaching experience that feels "aware" of the user's physical reality.

6. Multimodal Fusion Architectures for Mobile

To implement these advanced features—Computer Vision, Passive Sensing, and Generative AI—"myndmap" requires a sophisticated software architecture that fuses data from these disparate sources without draining the battery or violating user privacy.

6.1 Lightweight Model Selection: The "Edge AI" Stack

Given the real-time nature of these interventions and the need for privacy, processing must happen On-Device (Edge Computing).

  • Vision: MobileNetV3 or ShuffleNet are recommended backbones for the visual clutter and gaze models. These architectures are designed specifically for mobile efficiency, balancing accuracy with low parameter counts and latency.10

  • Object Detection: YOLO-Nano is the current industry standard for ultra-lightweight object detection, capable of running on mobile GPUs/NPUs (Neural Processing Units).9

  • Audio/Emotion: If voice features are implemented, DistilHuBERT or lightweight CNN-LSTMs provide robust emotion recognition with a fraction of the computational cost of full transformer models.74

6.2 Fusion Strategy: Late Fusion for Robustness

For an ADHD application, Late Fusion is the recommended architectural approach.76

  • Mechanism: In Late Fusion, each modality (Vision, Sensor, Text) is processed by its own independent model to produce a high-level feature vector or "score" (e.g., Vision model outputs "Clutter Score: 0.8"; Sensor model outputs "Restlessness Score: 0.4"). These scores are then concatenated and fed into a final "Decision Layer" (the RL Agent) to determine the intervention.

  • Advantages: This modularity is crucial for reliability. If the user covers the camera (blinding the Vision module) or denies microphone access, the Sensor and Text modules can still function, and the system can degrade gracefully rather than failing completely.77 It also allows for easier debugging and updating of individual components.

6.3 Privacy-First Architecture

The proposed system collects highly sensitive data: images of the user's home, behavioral patterns, and medication schedules. To ensure adoption and ethical compliance:

  • No Raw Data Egress: No images, video frames, or raw sensor logs should ever leave the user's device. Only the computed embeddings or scores (e.g., "Clutter Level: High") should be stored or processed.2

  • Federated Learning: If model improvements are needed, Federated Learning can be used to train the global model. The model updates are computed locally on the device and sent to the server, but the training data remains local.61

8.1 Feature Congestion Calculation (Python/OpenCV Pseudocode)

Context: Visual Clutter Detection utilizing Multi-scale Gaussian Pyramids 7

Python

import cv2
import numpy as np

def calculate_visual_clutter(image_path):
    # 1. Load image and convert to CIELab (Perceptual Color Space)
    # CIELab is used because it approximates human vision's sensitivity to lightness vs color.
    img = cv2.imread(image_path)
    lab_img = cv2.cvtColor(img, cv2.COLOR_BGR2Lab)
   
    # 2. Multi-scale Gaussian Pyramid (simulating visual processing)
    # The brain processes visual information at different spatial frequencies.
    # We create a pyramid to analyze the image at 3 different scales.
    pyramid = [lab_img]
    for i in range(2):
        pyramid.append(cv2.pyrDown(pyramid[-1]))
       
    # 3. Calculate Local Covariance (Feature Congestion)
    clutter_maps =
    for layer in pyramid:
        # Calculate gradients (orientation/contrast) using Sobel operators
        grad_x = cv2.Sobel(layer, cv2.CV_64F, 1, 0, ksize=3)
        grad_y = cv2.Sobel(layer, cv2.CV_64F, 0, 1, ksize=3)
        magnitude = cv2.magnitude(grad_x, grad_y)
       
        # Local variance calculation (entropy proxy)
        # High standard deviation in a local area implies high "congestion" or clutter.
        mean, std_dev = cv2.meanStdDev(magnitude)
        clutter_maps.append(std_dev)
       
    # 4. Aggregate Clutter Score
    # The final score is an aggregate of clutter across all scales.
    total_clutter_score = np.mean(clutter_maps)
   
    return total_clutter_score

8.2 Reinforcement Learning Reward Function Definition

Context: Notification Timing Optimization 59

The reward function $R(s, a)$ updates the Q-value for the notification agent, guiding it toward optimal timing strategies.

$$ R_t = \alpha \cdot \mathbb{I}(\text{ActionTaken}) + \beta \cdot \mathbb{I}(\text{TaskComplete}) - \gamma \cdot \mathbb{I}(\text{Dismissal}) - \delta \cdot \text{StressLevel} $$

Where:

  • $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is an indicator function (1 if true, 0 if false).

  • $\text{ActionTaken}$: The user engaged (tapped) the notification.

  • $\text{TaskComplete}$: The user marked the item as done within a threshold time $t$ (e.g., 30 mins).

  • $\text{Dismissal}$: The user swiped away the notification immediately (Negative Reward).

  • $\text{StressLevel}$: A continuous penalty derived from keystroke dynamics (flight time variability) at the time of interruption. This teaches the agent to avoid interrupting a stressed user.

  • $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$: Weighting coefficients that can be personalized (e.g., $\delta$ is higher for users with high anxiety sensitivity).
