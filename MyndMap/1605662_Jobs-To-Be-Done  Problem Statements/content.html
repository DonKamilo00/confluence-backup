TL;DR

  • Users "hire" Myndmap to do four core jobs: deconstruct overwhelming goals, create a reliable external system, help them initiate and complete tasks, and guide them in building better habits.

  • Understanding these "jobs" is more critical than listing features; it reveals why users need the product.

  • The biggest product opportunities lie in better serving these jobs, especially in overcoming the paralysis of task initiation.

Introduction to JTBD at Myndmap

The Jobs-To-Be-Done (JTBD) framework is central to the product strategy. It posits that users have "jobs" they need to get done in their lives and they "hire" products to help them. The focus is not on what features the product has, but on what progress the user is trying to make. By deeply understanding these underlying jobs, the team can design and build solutions that are genuinely valuable and drive long-term engagement. This approach is derived directly from the strategic analysis of user behavior and market needs.  

Core Job 1: "When I am facing a large, complex project, I want to deconstruct it into small, clear, actionable steps, so I can overcome overwhelm and know where to start."

Problem Statement: Users experience cognitive paralysis and emotional overwhelm when confronted with large, ambiguous goals, which prevents them from starting.

Evidence: The user data provides clear evidence of this struggle and the manual effort users expend to solve it. Users frequently create reminders with detailed, multi-step sub-tasks. For example, one user broke down the task "もう一度ちゃんと文章を読む” (read the text carefully again) into specific, timed sub-tasks: "色々とひまわりについて調べる15分" (research various things about sunflowers for 15 mins) and "要素を絵に描き出す30分" (draw out the elements in a picture for 30 mins). This manual decomposition is a clear signal of an unmet need. They are performing the "job" of task breakdown by hand, indicating a significant opportunity for a tool to automate and facilitate this process.  

Opportunity: The proposed "Al Smart Scheduler & Goal Decomposition" feature is the direct solution to this job. The AI's primary role should be to act as an "executive function assistant." It should not just create a list, but guide the user through the process of breaking down a large goal (e.g., "Pass university entrance exam") into a manageable project plan with scheduled sub-tasks. This directly reduces cognitive load and addresses the core pain point of initiation paralysis.  

Core Job 2: "When I am trying to manage my life, I want to create a reliable external system that mirrors my physical and mental space, so I can offload my working memory and trust that nothing will be forgotten."

Problem Statement: Users' internal systems for memory, organization, and context-switching are unreliable, leading to stress, anxiety, and missed commitments.

Evidence: The "Digital Twin" phenomenon observed in the organizationAreas data is the strongest evidence for this job. When users create areas like "本棚左側" (left side of bookshelf) or "玄関の靴箱上" (above the shoe box at the entrance), they are building a digital scaffold for their physical world. They are hiring the app to be their external memory, holding information tied to specific contexts. The sheer breadth of tasks managed within the app—from academic deadlines to rent payments to household chores—further illustrates its role as a centralized, external life management system.  

Opportunity: The immediate and most critical opportunity is to fix the geofenced reminders feature. Its failure to trigger breaks the user's trust in this external system precisely when it is needed most. Beyond this, there are opportunities to deepen the "digital twin" concept. This could include features like visual mapping of spaces, pre-built templates for organizing common areas (e.g., "Kitchen Organization Checklist"), or allowing users to attach photos to specific areas to enhance the connection between the digital and physical worlds.  

Core Job 3: "When I know what I need to do, I want help initiating, focusing on, and completing the task, so I can move from planning to doing."

Problem Statement: For many users, the primary bottleneck is not a lack of planning ability but the emotional and psychological difficulty of commencing a planned activity.

Evidence: The data reveals a seeming contradiction: users demonstrate a high capacity for creating detailed, well-structured plans and sub-task lists. Yet, "Task Initiation" is one of the most frequently cited primaryChallenges across all personas. This indicates that the core problem lies in overcoming the "activation energy" barrier—the initial resistance to starting. The cognitive work of planning is complete, but the emotional hurdle remains.  

This distinction is crucial for product development. It suggests that building more elaborate planning features may be less impactful than building features specifically designed to act as "starters." The product's role is not just to be a repository for plans, but an active partner in turning those plans into action.

Opportunity: This is a major area for AI-driven innovation. Instead of focusing solely on task decomposition, the AI can be designed to help users overcome their specific initiation barrier. This could manifest as:

  • Integrated Timers: A built-in Pomodoro timer that encourages users to commit to just a short burst of focused work.

  • AI-Suggested "First Steps": For a complex task, the AI could suggest an extremely small, low-friction "first easy step" to get the user started.

  • Encouraging Nudges: Gentle, non-shaming reminders for tasks that appear to be stalled, perhaps framed as questions (e.g., "Ready to spend 10 minutes on the research plan?").

Core Job 4: "When I want to improve myself, I want a simple way to build and track positive routines, so I can make consistent progress on my long-term goals."

Problem Statement: Users have a strong desire for self-betterment and habit formation but struggle with the consistency required to make lasting change.

Evidence: This job is supported by both cultural context and direct user behavior. The Japanese cultural value of kaizen, or continuous improvement, creates a fertile ground for tools that support personal growth. This is reflected in user-generated tasks focused on self-improvement, such as "マネジメントに役立つ本を30分読む!" (read a book useful for management for 30 mins!).  

However, the data also shows that conventional gamification may not be the right solution for this user base. Despite the common belief that points and badges are motivating for ADHD brains, the engagement with the userStats and userAchievements features is exceptionally low, with many profiles being "mostly empty" or "shells only". In contrast, engagement with core task management—the completion of tangible, real-world goals—is robust.  

This indicates that users are motivated by the intrinsic satisfaction of real-world accomplishment, not abstract in-app rewards. The "game" is not about earning points; it is about successfully completing a thesis, managing a household, or learning a new skill. Therefore, the product's motivational features should be designed to make this real progress more visible and satisfying.

Opportunity: The gamification strategy should be re-evaluated. Instead of adding abstract points or levels, focus on features that visualize tangible progress. This could include:

  • Goal-Specific Progress Bars: Visualizations showing progress toward a specific, user-defined goal (e.g., "Thesis Progress: 60% complete").

  • Meaningful Celebrations: Reinforce the completion of major projects or the achievement of significant milestones.

  • Robust Habit Tracking: A dedicated module that allows users to see their consistency over time through streaks and completion charts, directly appealing to the kaizen mindset.
